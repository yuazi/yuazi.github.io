The axe kick (naeryeo chagi) goes up and comes straight down. The leg rises above the opponent's head and the heel or sole drops onto the face or the top of the head guard. It looks slow on paper. In a match it is a standard head kick, because it comes over the top of a guard built to stop kicks from the side, and it scores three points.

It depends on flexibility more than most kicks. With tight hamstrings the leg stops at chest height and there is nothing left to drop.

---

## (y-) Mental Model

Think of the leg as an axe handle with the heel as the blade. The lift is only there to get the blade above the target. The scoring part is the drop, so the leg has to come down fast and in a straight line, pulled by the hip.

---

## (y-) Two Ways to Lift

| Version               | How it works                                                                                                    | Trade-off                                                                     |
| --------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Straight-leg lift     | The leg stays straight and swings up in an arc, either from inside to outside or outside to inside, then drops. | Easy to learn and powerful, but the big arc is visible from far away.         |
| Bent-knee (chambered) | The knee comes up tight to the chest like a front kick, then the leg extends above the head and drops.          | Faster and harder to read. Needs more active flexibility to extend that high. |

The bent-knee version off the front leg is the one you see most in sparring. It starts out looking like a front kick or a roundhouse chamber, so the opponent has a hard time reading it until the leg is already above their guard.

---

## (y-) The Step-by-Step

Bent-knee version, front (left) leg, from a sparring stance.

1. **Slide the rear foot up.** A small step brings your weight over the back foot so the front leg is free to lift.
2. **Chamber high.** Drive the left knee up toward your chest, higher than for a roundhouse. Keep the foot relaxed.
3. **Extend above the target.** Open the knee so the heel rises above the opponent's head. Lean back slightly to counterbalance, hands up.
4. **Pull the heel down.** Drive the leg down with the hip and hamstring. The heel or the sole lands on the face or the top of the head.
5. **Land in front.** Let the foot come down to the floor just ahead of you, in stance, ready for the next kick.

---

## (y-) Key Details

- Put the effort into the drop. A common habit is to work hard on the lift and then let the leg fall, which is slow and easy to step back from.
- Keep the support leg slightly bent. A locked support knee makes you tip back as the kicking leg goes up.
- Aim through the target, toward the chest. A heel that stops at face height has no follow-through and tends to just brush the head guard.
- The support heel can turn a little toward the target for reach, but the hips stay more square than in a roundhouse.

---

## (y-) Where It Wins in Sparring

The front-leg axe kick works best at close and middle range against someone who keeps their hands high and wide to stop roundhouses. It also works as a counter: when an opponent leans in to throw a body kick, their head moves forward under your rising leg. And it pairs naturally with the cut kick. Lift the front leg as if to block, and if they hesitate, keep lifting and bring it down as an axe.

A clinch is a bad place for it. You do not have space to get the leg above them and it leaves you on one foot in a crowd.

## (y-) Progression Drills

| Stage | Drill                                                                                                                               |
| ----- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1     | **Leg swings.** Straight-leg swings to the front, ten per leg, gradually higher. This is active flexibility, so keep it controlled. |
| 2     | **Wall-assisted chamber.** Hold a wall, chamber the knee high, extend up, hold for two seconds, then pull down fast.                |
| 3     | **Pad overhead.** Partner holds a paddle flat above head height. Hit it on the way down, not the way up.                            |
| 4     | **Disguise drill.** Throw the same chamber three times: once as a roundhouse, once as a front kick, once as an axe kick.            |
| 5     | **Cut-to-axe.** Partner throws a slow body kick. Jam it with a lifted front leg, then extend into an axe kick without landing.      |

## (y-) Common Mistakes

| Mistake                           | Fix                                                                          |
| --------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Leg falls instead of being pulled | Drive the heel down with the hamstring. Practice pulling down fast on a pad. |
| Kick stops at chest height        | This is a flexibility limit. See the front splits work below.                |
| Leaning far back                  | Lean only as much as the leg needs. Keep your eyes on the target, hands up.  |
| Big telegraphed arc               | Use the bent-knee version so the lift looks like other kicks.                |
| Landing far from the target       | Land the foot just in front of you so you are still in range to follow up.   |

The height of this kick comes from the hamstrings and the hip flexors of the support leg. The [[notes/taekwondo/front-splits|(y-) Front Splits]] and [[notes/taekwondo/warmup-routine|(y-) Flexibility Warm-Up]] pages cover that work.

> [!warning] Partner safety
> The heel comes down on the face. In drills, use a pad or kick light and controlled, and never drop the heel onto a partner's collarbone or the back of the neck.
