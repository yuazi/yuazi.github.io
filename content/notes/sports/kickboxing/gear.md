What to bring, and in what order to buy it. Most gyms lend gloves for the first few classes, so the only thing you really need on day one is a pair of wraps and the [[notes/sports/kickboxing/hand-wrapping|(y-) knowledge of how to put them on]].

---

## (y-) Buying order

| When             | What                                   | Why                                                                   |
| ---------------- | -------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| First class      | Hand wraps, water, a towel             | Gym gloves are shared and damp. Wraps are cheap and they're your own. |
| First month      | Your own gloves, a mouthguard          | Once you know you're staying. The mouthguard needs fitting at home.   |
| Before sparring  | Shin guards, groin guard, 16 oz gloves | Most gyms won't let you spar without them.                            |
| If your gym asks | Headgear                               | Rules differ between gyms and between amateur competitions.           |
| Whenever         | A skipping rope                        | Warm-ups and footwork rhythm at home.                                 |

## (y-) Gloves

Glove size is weight in ounces, and more ounces means more padding. For bag and pad work, 10 to 12 oz is common. For sparring, most gyms want 14 or 16 oz, partly to protect your hands and mostly to protect your partner's head. If you're only going to own one pair, buy 14 or 16 oz and use them for everything.

Pro fights use much smaller gloves. Glory's 2026 rules set 8 oz for weight classes under 65 kg and 10 oz for everyone else.

Velcro closures are the practical choice for training because you can put them on without help. Lace-up gloves fit better but need a training partner or coach to tie them.

Kickboxing gloves and boxing gloves are close to identical. Muay Thai gloves often have a slightly more flexible palm for catching kicks and clinching, which helps less under K-1 or Glory rules, where both are limited.

## (y-) Shin guards

Shin guards protect both people in sparring and in partner drills where kicks land on a body or leg. Look for ones that cover from just below the knee to the instep and don't rotate around the leg when you kick. There are two common types:

- Sock-style guards slide on like a sleeve. They're light and stay in place, with less padding.
- Strapped guards with a foam instep cover give more padding and some protection for the top of the foot, but they can shift if the straps loosen during a round.

Try a few kicks in them before buying, because a guard that spins on your shin ends up hitting your partner with its edge.

## (y-) Mouthguard

You need one before your first sparring round, no exceptions. A boil-and-bite guard from a sports shop is fine to start with. Follow the molding instructions and leave it in for the whole time it sets. A custom guard from a dentist fits better and makes breathing and talking easier, which matters more once you're sparring every week.

## (y-) Groin guard

Required for men in sparring, and Glory requires it in fights. A cup with a jockstrap or compression shorts holds it in place better than the cheap elastic-band versions.

## (y-) Care

- Air your gloves out after every session. Leaving them zipped in a bag is how they start to smell within a week.
- Wash wraps and mouthguard case often. Rinse the mouthguard after every use.
- Check the Velcro on gloves and shin guards every few months. Worn Velcro lets the guard slide down mid-round.
