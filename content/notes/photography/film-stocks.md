A film stock is a specific emulsion: a particular recipe of silver halide crystals, dyes and layers on a plastic base. Two stocks with the same ISO number can look very different in color, contrast, grain and how they handle bad light. Knowing the three broad families (color negative, slide, black and white) tells you how to expose and process a roll. Knowing individual stocks tells you what the result will look like. Stock lineups change often, with discontinuations, renamings and regional availability, so everything below is a general description, not a buying guide.

## Mental Model

- There are three families: color negative (processed in C-41), color slide or reversal (processed in E-6), and black-and-white negative.
- Negative film forgives overexposure much more than underexposure. Slide film forgives neither well.
- Faster film generally has coarser grain, because bigger crystals catch more light. Modern emulsions soften this trade-off but do not remove it.
- The stock sets the look before you touch a scanner. Color casts, contrast and grain structure are baked into the emulsion.

## 1. The Three Families

Color negative film records a scene as an inverted, color-reversed image with an orange tint from the film base (see [[notes/photography/scanning-film|Scanning Film]]). It is developed in the C-41 process ([[notes/photography/c41-color-developing|C-41 Color Developing]]) and is what most consumer and portrait film is. It has wide exposure latitude and a soft, gentle response in the highlights.

Slide film (also called reversal or transparency film) produces a positive image directly on the film. It is developed in E-6. You view it on a light table or project it, and what you see is what the camera recorded. It has higher contrast, usually higher saturation, and a narrower latitude than negative film, so the exposure has to be close.

Black-and-white negative film records brightness only. Because the image is made of metallic silver (not dye), you can develop it at home with simple chemistry ([[notes/photography/developing-black-and-white|Developing Black-and-White Film]]), and you can push or pull it freely ([[notes/photography/push-and-pull-processing|Push and Pull Processing]]). One exception: some B&W films, such as Ilford XP2 Super, are chromogenic and are developed in C-41 with dyes instead of silver.

## 2. Latitude

Latitude is how far you can miss the correct exposure and still get a usable image. The shape of the film's response curve explains the differences. Negative film has a long, gradual shoulder where highlights compress gently instead of clipping, and a toe in the shadows where detail drops out. Overexposure moves the scene up the curve, into the shoulder, where information survives. Underexposure pushes shadows below the toe, where there is nothing recorded to bring back.

That is why the usual advice for color negative is to give it a little more light than a meter alone suggests, and why [[notes/photography/metering-and-sunny-16|metering for the shadows]] works. As rough numbers, negative film is commonly described as tolerating one stop or so under and several stops over, while slide film is commonly treated as good to within about half a stop to a stop either way. Treat these as rules of thumb, not specifications, because they depend on the stock, the scene and what you call acceptable.

> [!tip] Intuition
> Think of the film as a bucket for light. A negative bucket has a sloped lip that lets overflow spill slowly. A slide bucket has a hard rim: once it is full, the highlights are gone.

## 3. Grain and Speed

Grain is the visible clumping of silver (in B&W) or dye clouds (in color). A faster film needs more sensitivity, and the crystals that gather more light are larger, so ISO 800 and 3200 films look grainier than ISO 100 and 125 films. Kodak's T-Max line and Ilford's Delta line use more modern crystal designs and show finer grain for their speed than the traditional films Tri-X and HP5 Plus, which many photographers like for exactly that texture.

Grain is not a flaw to avoid at all costs. Fine grain suits large prints and detail work, and coarse grain gives a feel that many people find pleasing in low light. What you should avoid is the surprise: if you plan to enlarge or scan at high resolution, match the stock to the output.

## 5. Color Balance and CineStill

Daylight film is balanced for light of about 5500 K. Under tungsten bulbs (about 3200 K), daylight film looks orange. Tungsten-balanced film, such as CineStill 800T, is designed for that light and looks neutral under it, with a cooler, blue-teal cast in daylight unless you use a filter.

CineStill's stocks are motion picture films (Kodak Vision3) that have been modified so they can be developed in standard C-41 chemistry. Motion picture negative carries a black remjet layer on the back, which absorbs light that would otherwise pass through the emulsion, reflect off the film base or pressure plate, and expose the film a second time. That second exposure is halation, and it shows as a red or orange glow around bright points such as street lamps. The remjet layer is removed in a separate step in the motion picture process (ECN-2) and does not survive C-41, so CineStill strips it before selling the film. Without it, halation returns, which explains the red halos you see around lights in night photographs from this stock.

Halation is a physical effect. Other stocks have a small amount of it too. It is only unusually visible in 800T because of the missing layer.

## 6. Choosing a Stock

A practical way to choose:

1. Decide what light you will shoot in. Bright daylight suits ISO 100 or 200. Mixed or dim light suggests ISO 400 and up.
2. Decide how much margin you want. If you are unsure of your exposure, color negative and HP5 Plus give the most room.
3. Decide on the final output. Scans that will be viewed small on a phone hide grain. Large prints reveal it.
4. Decide whether you will process yourself. B&W is easiest at home, C-41 needs temperature control, and E-6 is rarely done at home.

When you start, pick one color negative and one B&W stock, shoot several rolls of each in different light, and learn how they respond before collecting more.
