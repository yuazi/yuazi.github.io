"Kickboxing" covers several rulesets, and they change what's allowed more than the name suggests. Whether you can kick the legs, throw knees, or clinch at all depends on which one you're under. This page covers the ones you're most likely to see: the three amateur WAKO ring disciplines, Glory's professional rules, and Muay Thai for comparison.

---

## (y-) Where it comes from

The name is Japanese. Boxing promoter Osamu Noguchi coined "kickboxing" for a hybrid built mostly on Muay Thai with karate mixed in, and founded the first governing body, the Kickboxing Association, in 1966. Its first event was in Osaka that April. In the United States, full-contact karate grew into American kickboxing in the 1970s, with kicks kept above the waist. Japanese kickboxing and Muay Thai reached the Netherlands in the same decade, largely through Jan Plas, and Dutch gyms such as Mejiro Gym built a style around heavy punching combinations finished with low kicks. In 1993, Kazuyoshi Ishii founded K-1 in Japan. Its rules (punches, kicks, knees, no elbows, very little clinching) are the family most professional kickboxing still uses, Glory included. Glory was founded in 2012, after K-1's financial collapse.

## (y-) The rulesets side by side

| Ruleset                 | Leg kicks              | Knees | Elbows | Clinch                               | Rounds                                   |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------- | ----- | ------ | ------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------- |
| WAKO full contact       | No                     | No    | No     | No                                   | 3 × 2 min (senior, amateur)              |
| WAKO low kick           | Yes                    | No    | No     | No                                   | 3 × 2 min (senior, amateur)              |
| WAKO K-1 style          | Yes                    | Yes   | No     | Limited                              | 3 × 2 min (senior, amateur)              |
| Glory (pro, 2026 rules) | Yes, arcing kicks only | Yes   | No     | Only to throw one knee, then release | 3 × 3 min, 5 × 3 min for titles          |
| Muay Thai               | Yes                    | Yes   | Yes    | Full clinch, with sweeps             | Traditionally 5 × 3 min in Thai stadiums |

All of these have a one-minute break between rounds.

WAKO, the World Association of Kickboxing Organizations, runs amateur kickboxing. Its three ring disciplines are full contact, low kick and K-1 style, all fought with full power. It also has four tatami disciplines (point fighting, light contact, kick light, and forms), where techniques are controlled rather than full power.

## (y-) Glory rules in detail

Glory publishes its full rulebook. As of the March 16, 2026 version:

- **Legal strikes.** Punches with the padded part of the glove, the spinning back fist with the back of the glove, kicks to the legs, body and head (including spinning and jumping kicks), and knees, including knees to the legs.
- **Leg kicks.** Only arcing kicks (roundhouse or hooking motion) may target the legs. Front kicks, side kicks and back kicks to the legs are fouls.
- **Clinch.** You may grab only to immediately throw a single knee, and then you have to let go. Holding to rest, or to stop the other fighter from striking, is a foul.
- **Catching kicks.** You can grab a leg only to throw a single strike immediately, with one step in any direction, and then release it.
- **Fouls.** Elbows, head butts, strikes to the back of the head or the back, the groin, throws, sweeps, and pushing to off-balance someone.
- **Gloves.** 8 oz under 65 kg, 10 oz for all other weight classes.

## (y-) How Glory fights are scored

Five judges (sometimes three) score each round on a 10-point must system, and the scores are made public after every round. They look at four things, in this order:

1. **Knockdowns.** A knockdown wins the round, unless the fighter who scored it loses points to fouls.
2. **Damage.** High-impact damage (a wobble, a stagger, a fighter who can't function the same way afterwards) counts more than anything else.
3. **Clean strikes.** The number of clean hits. Spinning and flying techniques count for more than ordinary ones.
4. **Aggression and ring control.** Attacking counts for more than defending.

A normal round goes 10-9. A knockdown, or high-impact damage with clear domination, makes it 10-8, and two knockdowns make it 10-7. In tournament fights, a fighter knocked down twice in one round or three times in one fight loses by TKO. In a tournament final, the limits are three in a round or four in the fight.

A fighter who goes down gets a mandatory eight count. The referee can also give a standing eight count to a fighter who's still on their feet but in trouble.

## (y-) What this means for training

- Low kicks and checking are legal in almost every pro ruleset, so they belong in every session. See [[notes/kickboxing/low-kicks|(y-) Low Kicks & Checking]].
- The clinch is a single knee and out, so don't spend time on long Muay Thai clinch work unless you're also training Muay Thai.
- Judges score damage first, so pad and bag work should train hard, clean strikes rather than lots of light ones.
- Spinning techniques get a bonus. The [[notes/taekwondo/spinning-kicks|(y-) taekwondo spinning kicks]] are worth keeping.
